  • AKA: Environmental Music, Kankyō Ongaku, Furniture Music // Mid 70´s

Ambient is music built around atmosphere rather than melody or rhythm — sound designed to shape a space instead of demanding attention. Its founding idea predates the name by half a century: in 1917 Erik Satie composed what he called musique d'ameublement ("furniture music"), pieces written for intermissions and meant deliberately not to be listened to. The technical means arrived later, through the musique concrète and electronic studios of Paris, Cologne and Milan, which established recorded sound itself as compositional material, and through American minimalism — La Monte Young's sustained tones, Terry Riley's "A Rainbow in Curved Air" (1969) — which replaced development with duration and slow change.


Much of the music arrived before the label did, and it came from Germany. Klaus Schulze's "Irrlicht" (1972) is essentially an hour of organ and orchestral drone, while "Timewind" (1975) stretches two sequencer pieces across a full LP. Tangerine Dream's "Phaedra" (1974) and "Rubycon" (1975) work the same territory. Within the Düsseldorf scene, Cluster moved from harsh electronics toward something gentle and pastoral on "Zuckerzeit" (1974) and "Sowiesoso" (1976), and Harmonia — Moebius, Roedelius and Michael Rother — did much the same on "Musik von Harmonia" (1974) and "Deluxe" (1975). The Berlin School's emphasis on texture and space over rhythm makes it the most direct ancestor of the genre.


Brian Eno supplied the name. Bedridden after being hit by a taxi in 1975, he was left with a harp record playing too quietly to hear properly, half-buried under the sound of rain, and heard in it a different way of listening. "Discreet Music" (1975) came directly from that experience, built on a tape-delay system largely left to run itself. The term itself appeared on "Ambient 1: Music for Airports" (1978), where Eno's sleeve note defined the genre as music that accommodates many levels of attention without enforcing any of them — as ignorable as it is interesting. The series continued with "The Plateaux of Mirror" (1980) with Harold Budd, "Day of Radiance" (1980) by Laraaji, and the darker, more geographic "On Land" (1982). Eno also went to Germany: "Cluster & Eno" (1977) and "After the Heat" (1978) are the point where the two lineages meet directly.


A parallel tradition developed in Japan under the name kankyō ongaku ("environmental music"), often commissioned for buildings, shops and exhibitions rather than for records. Hiroshi Yoshimura's "Music for Nine Post Cards" (1982) was written for a museum, and Satoshi Ashikawa, Midori Takada and Yasuaki Shimizu worked in related directions. Most of it went unnoticed outside Japan until reissues in the 2010s made it central to the canon. Elsewhere the genre divided by temperament: Steve Roach's "Structures from Silence" (1984) and "Dreamtime Return" (1988) established a long-form cosmic strand, Eliane Radigue built extraordinarily slow works on the ARP 2500, Jon Hassell proposed a "Fourth World" music of imaginary geography and processed trumpet, and Lustmord's "Heresy" (1990) crystallized dark ambient out of industrial music.


The rave scene gave ambient a second life. Chill-out rooms needed music for coming down, and The KLF's "Chill Out" (1990) and The Orb's "The Orb's Adventures Beyond the Ultraworld" (1991) supplied it, adding dub bass, found samples and humour. Aphex Twin's "Selected Ambient Works 85–92" (1992) and the far colder "Volume II" (1994) pushed further, and Global Communication's "76:14" (1994), Biosphere's "Substrata" (1997) and Wolfgang Voigt's Gas project ("Zauberberg", 1997; "Pop", 2000) made the decade arguably the genre's richest.


Since 2000 the drone and modern-classical strands have dominated, through William Basinski's "The Disintegration Loops" (2002), Stars of the Lid's "And Their Refinement of the Decline" (2007), and the work of Tim Hecker, Grouper and Kali Malone. At the same time, streaming has turned ambient into infrastructure — endless focus and sleep playlists — which is either the complete fulfilment of Satie's original premise or its final trivialisation, depending on who is asked.
